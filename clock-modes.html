FIELD NOTES / 001 · 14 NOVEMBER 2026

Let the GPU rest.

A fixed clock helped us test one idea. Adaptive mode let us explore another: lower clocks at idle, with a ceiling under load.

Two modes, two different requests

FIXED LOCK

1,800 → 1,800 MHz

Requests the same minimum and maximum. This is the original tester’s fallback. Actual reported clocks can differ slightly.

ADAPTIVE CAPPED

300 → 2,200 MHz

Requests a range. The driver can adjust the clock with workload and other constraints. This mode is experimental.

The app also allows adaptive minimum requests from 100–300 MHz and caps including 1,800 and 1,900 MHz, up to 2,200 MHz where supported. Those choices are not recommendations for every laptop. Voltage stays automatic, and the driver can round or reject requests.

One short test, in numbers

The recording on 14 November 2026 ran from 00:55:00 to 00:58:43, with 223 samples and roughly 40 seconds of substantial activity while a model was training. The GPU was an RTX 5070 Ti Laptop GPU, reporting driver 610.74.

The recording contains no incident markers or long sampling gaps. Some samples report NVIDIA’s software power-cap flag, meaning power-related clock limiting. That flag is not a crash report. Memory temperature and the configured power limit were unavailable.

What we can—and can’t—conclude

In this recording, the GPU ramped up for work and dropped back to low-power idle. That is the behaviour we wanted to observe. A short run cannot establish long-term stability or prove that previous TDR crashes are solved.

The training summaries recorded 40.170 and 34.767 seconds with matching reported settings and losses. They cover 335 examples, three epochs, a batch size of eight and 126 optimiser updates. Without repeated controlled runs, we cannot attribute the time difference solely to clock settings.

Trying a profile thoughtfully

  1. Read the project’s setup and recovery instructions. Keep a record of your current settings.
  2. Choose a clock mode and change one thing at a time. Press Apply; editing a number alone does not apply it.
  3. Watch actual readings during work and when returning to idle. An accepted request is not a stability certificate.
  4. Only confirm a profile for startup after you have manually tested it. Each user confirms their own profile.
  5. Use Reset to restore owned settings and disable startup reapplication. Read any failure message instead of bypassing recovery.
There is no “keep forcing it” loop.

Startup waits for driver readiness and makes one application attempt per sign-in. Monitoring reads telemetry; it does not repeatedly apply the lock. A failure pauses automatic reapplication for manual review.

Evidence and further reading

These observations come from the original tester’s telemetry CSV, Governor action log and two training summaries. Raw private logs are not distributed. The project README records the test’s scope and limitations. NVIDIA documents the clock-event flags here.
